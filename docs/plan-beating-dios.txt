Where you can beat DIOS

Each claim says why it's believable and what's hard. DIOS already claims explanations, automatic replanning and scenarios, so you win on depth and speed, not on having the feature.

1. Replan in seconds, with a stable planRe-solve only what a change touches, starting from the current plan.

Why believable: warm starts, fixing untouched variables and CP-SAT neighbourhood search re-solve a small window, not 1.4M variables.

Your metric: a plan stability score (share of orders that moved) shown next to every replan, so planners trust it.

Hard: keeping a local fix close to the global optimum. Run a full re-solve nightly and compare.

2. A why on every decisionWhich limit made this order late, and what would fix it.

Why believable: MILP gives binding constraints and shadow prices; infeasible models give an irreducible subset you can name in plain words.

Beyond DIOS: one-click counterfactuals ("add a Saturday shift") solved as a scenario and diffed against the plan.

Hard: duals don't exist for integer models; you re-solve the fixed LP, which takes care to explain honestly.

3. An LLM copilot around the solverIt drafts mappings and constraints and answers questions; the solver still decides.

Why believable: OptiGuide and ORLM show LLMs answering what-ifs and drafting OR models. DIOS keeps AI chat out of its core.

Competition: Timefold already sells a Copilot that answers questions about a plan, so this alone isn't unique.

Hard: drafted constraints are often wrong. Nothing reaches production without passing its unit tests.

4. Weeks to go live, not a consulting projectTemplates plus an AI-assisted data mapper plus a data-quality score.

Why believable: Makkook's own deck lists workshops, a pilot, tuning, delivery and go-live, which is a services model.

Your version: load the ERP extract, get a mapped model and a scored data report in a day, a first plan in a week.

Hard: client data is messy everywhere. The template library takes years of real plants to mature.

5. A digital twin that tests the plan firstSimulate breakdowns and late trucks before you commit.

Why believable: salabim runs a plan as events; 200 random runs give a robustness score per plan.

Beyond DIOS: pick between two plans by how they survive bad days, not only by cost on paper.

Hard: the twin is only as good as its calibration to the real floor.

6. Cheaper and on-prem by defaultNo per-core solver fees unless a client wants them.

Why believable: the default stack is MIT, Apache and BSD; commercial solvers plug in per client.

Regional edge: on-prem with self-hosted models via vLLM suits plants that won't send data to a cloud.

Hard: Makkook has six implementations, a funded team and an academic optimisation lead. You're behind on references.

What's genuinely hardThe parts that decide whether this works.

Model correctness: a fast plan that breaks a real rule loses the client. Every constraint needs a test.

SAP: PyRFC is archived; you'll write OData and BAPI adapters and need SAP access to test them.

Scale on free solvers: 1.4M variables in one MILP is beyond HiGHS in minutes. Decomposition is mandatory, not optional.
